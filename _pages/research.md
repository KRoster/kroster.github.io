## 1. Preserving the useful lifespan of antibiotics

Antimicrobial resistance is an urgent global health threat. New drugs promise to prevent
infections and offer treatment alternatives for multi-drug-resistant infections. However, there
is limited evidence on how best to introduce these new interventions in rapidly evolving
epidemiologic and behavioral settings. Without a clearer understanding of how different
deployment strategies impact disease incidence and antimicrobial resistance, we may fail to
maximize new interventions' potential and risk the continued spread of resistance.

In my work, I simulate different strategies for the introduction of new interventions to maximize
their impact and stop the spread of resistance.

## 2. Designing surveillance systems to detect hidden patterns

When the first cases of a new pathogen or a new drug-resistant strain are detected, the data
needed to respond do not yet exist. In our work, we ask what can be inferred from those first
observations: how many infections went undetected? Is transmission likely to continue? And which
surveillance strategies detect emerging threats soonest?

## 3. Prediction where the data are thin

Forecasts are most valuable exactly where the historical record is shortest, for example in
emerging outbreaks or settings with limited surveillance capacity. This line of research asks how
much predictive signal can be borrowed from elsewhere: from meteorological and epidemiological
covariates, from other geographies, and from well-observed endemic diseases to novel pathogens.

## 4. Why trends change: identifying drivers of shifting disease burden

A change in reported cases can reflect a change in transmission, in care-seeking and diagnostic
capacity, or in the surveillance system itself — and the policy response differs in each case.
This work asks how to attribute observed change to its possible drivers: whether the decline in
dengue cases during the COVID-19 pandemic reflected reduced transmission or reduced
ascertainment, why gonorrhea case rates in the United States are falling, and whether geographic
variation in antibiotic prescribing is driven by underlying disease prevalence or by prescriber
behavior.
