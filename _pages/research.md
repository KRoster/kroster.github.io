## Preserving the useful lifespan of antibiotics

Antimicrobial resistance is an urgent global health threat. New drugs promise to prevent infections and offer treatment alternatives for multi-drug-resistant infections. However, there
is limited evidence on how best to introduce these new interventions in rapidly evolving epidemiologic and behavioral settings. Without a clearer understanding of how different deployment strategies impact disease incidence and antimicrobial resistance, we may fail to maximize new interventions' potential and risk the continued spread of resistance.

In my work, I simulate different strategies for the introduction of new interventions to maximize their impact and stop the spread of resistance.

## Disentangling changes in reporting and disease incidence

A change in reported cases can reflect a change in transmission, in care-seeking and diagnostic
capacity, or in the surveillance system itself. In this line of research, I aim to attribute changes in observed case counts to possible drivers, for example to understand whether the decline in dengue cases during the COVID-19 pandemic reflected reduced transmission or reduced ascertainment, why gonorrhea case rates in the United States are falling, or whether geographic variation in antibiotic prescribing is driven by underlying disease prevalence or by prescriber behavior.

## Designing surveillance systems to detect hidden patterns

When the first cases of a new pathogen or a new drug-resistant strain are detected, the data
needed to respond do not yet exist. In our work, we ask what can be inferred from those first
observations: how many infections remain undetected? Is transmission likely to continue? And which
surveillance strategies detect emerging threats soonest?

## Prediction where the data are limited

Forecasts are most valuable and most challenging where data are scarce, for example in emerging outbreaks or settings with limited surveillance capacity. To improve forecasts, I investigate where predictive signal can be transferred across geographies, pathogens, and datasets, for example to predict the spread of Zika from dengue data.
